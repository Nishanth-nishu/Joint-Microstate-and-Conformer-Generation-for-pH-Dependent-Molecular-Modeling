What limits Torsional Diffusion on QM9, and what FlexiTors has to learn

Torsional Diffusion only moves the rotatable torsions; it keeps every bond length, bond angle and ring shape from RDKit. Round 1 measured what that costs. Round 2 tests how to give the model better bond geometry. This page covers the results, why each experiment was run, and what comes next.

Round 1 results

Each arm changes one thing about what the model is given. All values are AMR-R, the mean over true conformers of the distance to the closest generated one. Lower is better. The dashed line is the TorDiff baseline we reproduced (0.176 Å, against 0.178 Å in the paper).

The pipeline reproduces the paper, so the ablations can be trusted

Why I did it

Every later number is a difference from the baseline. If our baseline were off, every ablation would be off with it.

What we gained

Baseline 0.176 Å vs 0.178 Å in the paper. Our own retrained model: 0.179 Å. Passing true conformers through the evaluator gives exactly 0.000 Å.

What it showed

The paper's 92.8% coverage can't be reproduced, even from its own released conformers (88.8%). 65–69 molecules fail in RDKit and count as zero. The paper never reports failures.

What to do with it

Compare only against our own numbers. Report failure counts and coverage on molecules every arm handled, alongside the protocol number.

Bond geometry is about half of TorDiff's error (A1, A2, A3)

Why I did it

FlexiTors rests on the claim that frozen RDKit bond geometry is the bottleneck. A1 swaps in the true geometry and keeps everything else, to measure that claim directly.

What we gained

A1: −0.093 Å [−0.099, −0.086]. A2 (MMFF before diffusion): −0.026 Å [−0.031, −0.021]. A3 (MMFF after): +0.010 Å worse on recall, −0.057 Å better on precision.

What it showed

Most of the remaining error is geometry the model can't change. Relaxing afterwards pulls conformers into nearby minima: more precise, less diverse. Stereo (G1) has no effect.

What to do with it

Geometry has to be right during generation, not patched after. MMFF-before is a free baseline any FlexiTors result must beat.

Where the geometry error lives

Research agent A split the torsion-oracle floor by ring size. The floor is the error left even with perfect torsions, so only a change in bond geometry can remove it. The verifier reproduced these numbers exactly.

Ring geometry, not acyclic angles, carries the floor on QM9

Why I did it

The obvious FlexiTors design diffuses non-ring bond angles together with torsions. Before building it, I needed to know how much of the error that design could ever reach.

What we gained

Acyclic molecules: true angles and bond lengths take the floor from 0.051 to 0.008 Å. Ring molecules, 72% of conformers: the same fix only goes from 0.117 to 0.099 Å.

What it showed

An acyclic-angle factor reaches only a small part of the error. Rings of 7 or more atoms are the worst (0.218 Å), then 5-rings (0.212 Å). The verifier corrected A's "5-rings worst".

What to do with it

FlexiTors needs a ring component (puckering coordinates, as in PuckerFlow) or a full-geometry refiner. Round-2 arm S1 tests ring-only vs acyclic-only true geometry with the model in the loop.

The B1 crossover

B1 is TorDiff retrained on the true bond geometry of the training set, not RDKit's. It is the clearest result of round 1.

A model trained on true geometry is the best model, and the most brittle

Why I did it

To check whether the model can use accurate geometry when it gets it, and how much it depends on the geometry it was trained on (gap G2: train/test shift).

What we gained

On true geometry, B1 scores 0.037 vs 0.084 Å for the standard model (−0.047, and +23 points of coverage at 0.05 Å). On RDKit geometry it scores 0.234 vs 0.181 Å, about as bad as no model at all (0.230).

What it showed

The torsion model is tuned to the geometry distribution it saw in training. TD reports the same on GEOM-DRUGS (Table 8: 0.920 vs 0.922). It may also read torsion hints from the geometry. That is unverified; round 2 tests it.

What to do with it

Learning the geometry is worth up to 0.14 Å here, but the torsion model must tolerate imperfect geometry. Round-2 arms S2, S3 and S4 train for that tolerance. S1 measures how accurate a learned geometry must be before B1 wins.

TorDiff's conformer-matching recipe is already close to right

Why I did it

Conformer matching is how TorDiff hides the gap between RDKit and true geometry during training. B2 and B5 test whether a different matching would help.

What we gained

Both alternatives were slightly worse (+0.002 and +0.006 Å). Regenerating the pickles ourselves changed nothing that matters (−0.002 Å).

What it showed

Tuning the matching step leaves almost nothing on the table. The real gain is in the geometry itself (B1 on true geometry).

What to do with it

Stop tuning matching. Spend compute on giving the model better geometry and on making it robust to geometry error.

Threshold saturation

At 0.5 Å every method looks alike; the differences sit at 0.05–0.25 Å

Why I did it

Research B flagged that newer papers (S23D, FM-refiner) treat QM9 at 0.5 Å as saturated and report much finer thresholds.

What we gained

MMFF-before cut error by 15% but changed coverage at 0.5 Å by only +0.05 points. At 0.1 Å it added 12 points; true geometry added 43.

What it showed

Coverage at 0.5 Å can't see the improvements FlexiTors targets. AMR-R can, and so can fine-threshold coverage.

What to do with it

You fixed 0.5 Å as the single primary threshold, so it stays primary. COV-R@0.1 on molecules every arm handles is a pre-declared secondary metric with its own correction. Say if you want it promoted.

What I think the results show

The bottleneck on QM9 is bond geometry, and mostly ring geometry. Torsions are not the problem. With true geometry, even random torsions (0.157 Å) beat TorDiff on RDKit geometry (0.176 Å). The torsion model is good; it is fed the wrong scaffold.

  • FlexiTors is justified, but the obvious version is aimed at the wrong target. Diffusing acyclic bond angles with torsions reaches a small part of the error. The design needs rings: a puckering factor or a full-geometry refiner.
  • Robustness matters as much as accuracy. B1 shows that a torsion model trained on perfect geometry breaks on imperfect geometry. A learned geometry generator will never be perfect, so the torsion model must be trained to tolerate its errors. That is what S2–S4 test.
  • Physics is a strong, cheap baseline. MMFF before diffusion gives 15% for free. Any FlexiTors result has to beat it, and S5 makes it stronger still.
  • The headline coverage number is the wrong scoreboard. At 0.5 Å, QM9 hides the effects that matter, and the paper's own 92.8% doesn't reproduce. AMR-R and fine thresholds carry the signal.
  • Coupling between torsions and geometry (G3) is small on QM9 for the standard model (−0.004 Å), larger for B1 (−0.016 Å, an oracle upper bound partly due to sampling). Expect it to matter more on GEOM-DRUGS, where TD's own appendix shows 0.324 → 0.284 Å.

Next steps

  • Now: round 2 is training on gnode118 (13 runs, about 2–2.5 days). Each model is evaluated automatically when its training ends.
  • Decision after round 2: if S2 or S4 matches TorDiff on RDKit geometry and beats it on better geometry, the torsion side of FlexiTors is solved. Then build the geometry generator to the accuracy S1 says is needed.
  • Round 3 candidates: a ring-puckering factor (PuckerFlow-style) composed with TorDiff; a learned geometry refiner; joint acyclic-angle diffusion only if S1 shows acyclic geometry matters with the model in the loop.
  • Then GEOM-DRUGS, where coupling and ring diversity are larger and the published baselines are less saturated.
  • Open question for you: whether COV-R@0.1 becomes a co-primary metric. It stays secondary, as you pre-registered.
